# Measurement

Most people measure the wrong thing, conclude the wrong thing, and then change the
wrong thing. This file is how not to.

---

## 1. There are three scoreboards, and they disagree

| Scoreboard | What it measures | What wins it |
|---|---|---|
| **REACH** | how many people saw it | humour, memes, dialogue hooks, meta-content, short posts |
| **RESONANCE** | how many of them cared | data, lists, reader-callouts, number hooks, comment-gated assets |
| **REVENUE** | how many became customers | tactical utility posts, proof posts, and the profile they land on |

**These pull in different directions.** In the measured data, the list of formats
that won reach and the list that won engagement barely overlapped. Humour and
dialogue buy *attention*. Analytical and specific posts buy *response*.

**Pick which one a post is for before you write it.** A utility post judged on reach
looks like a failure. A meme judged on leads looks like a failure. Both judgements
are wrong, and both lead to deleting the thing that was working.

**The most expensive mistake in this whole pack:** step-by-step how-to posts had the
*worst* reach of any format measured — and they are consistently the biggest driver
of clicks, signups and actual leads. If you judge them by impressions, you will kill
your best lead source and never know.

---

## 2. The three signals that predict a lead

Likes do not predict revenue. These three do, and you can see all of them without
any tooling:

**1. Bookmarks / saves.** Someone saving your post means they intend to *use* it.
That is the closest thing to intent the platform gives you for free.

**2. Shares.** Someone putting their own name behind your idea.

**3. Comments where someone tags a colleague.** The single strongest signal there is.
"@Sarah we should do this" is a person starting an internal conversation about your
work. That is a buying process beginning in public.

**The monthly ritual:** export your top posts, and rank them by these three — not by
likes. Then make more of whatever is at the top. A post with 30 likes and four
tag-a-colleague comments is worth more than a post with 600 likes and none.

## 3. Compute your own impressions-to-lead ratio

Everybody quotes someone else's number. Yours is different and you can calculate it.

```
(impressions over 90 days) ÷ (leads that mentioned or came from LinkedIn in 90 days)
= impressions per lead
```

Reported ratios from real B2B accounts range from roughly **3,000 to 10,000
impressions per booked call**, depending on offer, price point and audience quality.
That range is too wide to borrow. Measure your own.

Once you have it, content stops being a feeling and becomes arithmetic:

> "We need 8 more calls a month. At our ratio that's roughly X impressions.
> Our median post does Y. So that's Z more posts, or better posts."

That sentence is what gets a content budget approved. It is also what stops a founder
asking "is this working?" every fortnight.

## 4. Attribution: you need both halves

**First touch — ask every prospect.** Put one question on every sales call and every
form: *"How did you first hear about us?"*

This is the only way to capture the path no tool can see: a VC saw your work, sent it
to a portfolio company, they called you three weeks later. No dashboard will ever
show you that. The prospect will tell you if you ask.

Log the answers verbatim in one sheet. After a hundred of them you will know things
about your business that no analytics tool can tell you.

**Last touch — tag every link.** Every link out of LinkedIn gets a UTM tag:
- the link in your bio
- the link in your featured section
- the link in your company page
- the link in every first comment

Without tags, all of that traffic lands in your analytics as "direct" and becomes
invisible. Tagging costs five minutes once.

**Neither half is enough alone.** First touch tells you where they met you. Last
touch tells you where they came from. They are usually different places, and the gap
between them is where most content budgets get wrongly cut.

## 5. The founder profile and the company page do different jobs

A pattern that holds across accounts:

- **The founder profile is almost entirely top of funnel.** It makes people curious.
  It gets almost all of the reach.
- **The company page is where they check you out.** It gets a fraction of the
  impressions — and a disproportionate share of the people who actually book.

The path is: see the founder → get curious → look up the company → convert.

**What follows:** do not judge the company page by its impressions. It is not a reach
channel, it is a *verification* channel. Keep it current, keep it credible, and make
sure the link works — but stop measuring it like the founder profile.

And optimise the profile itself, not just the posts. See `13-PROFILE-FOUNDATION.md`.

## 6. The self-audit — the most valuable 45 minutes you will spend

**Do not analyse someone else's account. Analyse your own.** Outside benchmarks are
a starting point. Your own top 30% is the answer.

Run this monthly once you have 30+ posts:

**Step 1. Export.** LinkedIn's analytics gives you your top posts by impressions.
Download it. Get 50 rows if you can.

**Step 2. Keep the top 30%.** Discard the rest. Never analyse average output —
yours or anyone's. The tail is noise.

**Step 3. Classify each one.** Add columns:
hook type (from `04-HOOK-GUIDE.md`) · format (from `06-POST-STRUCTURE.md`) ·
media type · character count · topic · saves · shares · tag-a-colleague comments

**Step 4. Do the same for the bottom 30%.** The losers teach more than the winners,
because the winners are contaminated by luck.

**Step 5. Ask five questions.**
- What do the top 30% have in common that the bottom 30% do not?
- Which hook type appears most in the top? Least in the bottom?
- Is there a length pattern?
- Which posts produced the three lead signals, regardless of impressions?
- Which topic reliably underperforms? (Stop posting it.)

**Step 6. Change exactly one thing next month.** Not five. One. Then re-run this and
see whether it moved.

`prompts/self-audit.md` will run steps 3–5 for you if you paste in the export.

> **This is how the whole system was built in the first place.** Not from theory —
> from getting 100 posts out, seeing what the winners had in common, seeing what the
> losers had in common, and doing more of the first thing. There is no shortcut past
> shipping enough posts to have data.

## 7. Two traps that will fool you

**Trap 1: paid promotion hides inside your "organic" numbers.**

If you ever boost a post — LinkedIn's thought-leader ads let you promote your own
organic content — those impressions appear inside your normal analytics. Your top
posts list becomes a **spend ranking**, not a popularity ranking.

In the measured account, over a quarter of all reach was paid, concentrated on a
handful of posts. Reading that list naively said "post more product content." With
the paid stripped out, product content was one of the *worst* performing categories.
Acting on the naive number would have meant doing more of something purely on the
strength of money spent, not interest earned.

**Always subtract paid before drawing a conclusion.** If you cannot subtract it,
exclude boosted posts from the analysis entirely.

**Trap 2: reach can go up while resonance falls.**

In the measured year, organic reach more than doubled while the engagement rate
roughly halved. More people were seeing it; a smaller share cared. That is what
buying and borrowing reach looks like — through ads, through broader top-of-funnel
content, through humour — outrunning the audience's actual appetite.

**Track the ratio, not just the top-line number.** Reach going up is not automatically
good news.

## 8. Worked example: when your best posts are your worst posts

A real design agency's account, five months, 66 original posts.

**The top performers by engagement** were revenue-milestone posts — "we doubled our
revenue," "highest month ever," "2023 vs 2026." They scored two to four times the
median.

**And they were the wrong posts.** The engagement came overwhelmingly from peers,
students, and the founder's home market — not from the enterprise buyers in another
country that the business actually sells to.

Meanwhile the tactical posts — teardowns of what makes a B2B site work, contrarian
takes on design process — sat near the bottom on engagement and are exactly the posts
that make a founder look like an expert worth calling.

**The lesson, and it applies to almost every account:**

> Your highest-engagement post and your highest-value post are frequently different
> posts. If your audience does not match your buyer, engagement is actively
> misleading — it will pull you toward writing for the wrong crowd, one well-performing
> post at a time.

Two practical rules from this:
1. **Cap the money and milestone posts at one or two a month.** They work, they just
   work on the wrong people. They are seasoning, not the meal.
2. **Check *who* engaged, not just how many.** Click into the reactions list. If the
   names are not buyers, that post did not do the job, whatever the number says.

## 9. When to conclude anything

- **Under 90 days:** conclude nothing. The account is not readable yet.
- **Under 30 posts:** conclude nothing about format or hook type. The sample is noise.
- **After a single viral post:** conclude nothing. One outlier week will distort every
  correlation you run. Remove it and check whether your finding survives. Frequently
  it does not.
- **Falling reach with a steady engagement rate:** this is an account-level
  distribution problem, not a content problem. Diagnose it as a penalty or an
  algorithm change, not as bad writing.

And the honest ceiling on all of this: in the measured account, weekly reach and
inbound demos correlated positively but weakly — reach explained under 10% of the
week-to-week variation. **LinkedIn is one input among many, not the dial that sets
inbound.** Anyone claiming a strong causal link from this kind of data is overreading it.
