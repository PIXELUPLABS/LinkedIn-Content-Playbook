# Media and Images

For a business that sells visual work, this is not a side question. **This is the
whole channel.**

---

## 1. The finding that should change your week

A design agency's own LinkedIn account was measured over five months — 66 original
posts, scored as likes + (2 × comments) + (3 × reposts).

| What was attached | Typical performance |
|---|---|
| **Video** | Best — around 4x the text-only median |
| **Image** | Around 3x the text-only median |
| **Text only** | Worst by a wide margin |

**Text-only posts were roughly a quarter as effective as posts with something to
look at.** Not slightly worse. Multiples worse.

An independent study of 559 posts from three B2B creators found the same ordering:
document/carousel posts performed best, then video, then plain text.

**The rule that follows: never post text alone.** For a design business it is close
to malpractice — the product is the picture, and you left it out.

## 3. The image rules

**1. The hook goes in the image too.**
Someone scrolling with sound off and text collapsed should still get the point from
the first frame. Put the headline in the image. This is the single highest-return
design decision on the platform, and almost nobody selling design does it.

**2. First slide is 80% of a carousel's job.**
It is a hook with a picture. Treat it with the same seriousness as the first line of
the post. Big type, one idea, readable at thumbnail size.

**3. Design for a thumbnail, not a monitor.**
Most people see your image at roughly the size of a stamp before they decide. If the
idea is not legible small, the idea is not there.

**4. Square or portrait. Never landscape.**
Portrait takes more vertical space in the feed, which means more of the screen and
more time in view. 4:5 or 1:1. Landscape wastes your best asset.

**5. Real beats rendered — sometimes.**
For proof, process and transparency: real screenshots, real photos, real mess. For
craft drops and curation: fully designed. Matching these wrong is a common mistake —
a beautifully designed "here are our numbers" graphic reads as an ad.

**6. Never a stock photo.** Ever. One stock photo undoes a year of taste signalling.

**7. Show the rejected work.**
Counter-intuitive and consistently strong. The versions you did not ship prove
judgement in a way the final version cannot — anyone can show a finished thing;
showing what you chose *against* is the actual skill.

## 4. Carousel anatomy

The highest-value format available to a visual business, and the most under-used.

```
Slide 1   THE HOOK.        Big type. One idea. Nothing else.
Slide 2   THE STAKES.      Why this matters, one sentence.
Slides 3–8  ONE IDEA EACH. Never two. A slide with two ideas is two slides.
Slide 9   THE SUMMARY.     All the points on one screen — this is the slide people screenshot.
Slide 10  THE ASK.         One line. Who you are, what you do, one soft next step.
```

- 8–12 slides. Under 8 feels thin, over 12 loses people.
- **The same type size throughout.** Varying it reads as a deck, not a story.
- **Number the slides.** "3/9" tells people how much is left, which keeps them going.
- **Slide 9 is the one that gets saved.** Design it last and design it hardest.

## 5. Video

Video was the top performer in the agency data measured for this pack, and it is the
one most teams skip because it feels expensive.

It is not expensive:
- **Screen recording of work in motion** — a hover state, a transition, a prototype.
  15 seconds, no face, no editing. This is most of what you should post.
- **Before/after wipe.** Old site, new site, one cut.
- **A 30-second answer to a real question**, filmed on a phone.

Rules:
- **First 3 seconds decide.** Start on the most interesting frame, not on a title card.
- **Assume no sound.** Burn in captions.
- **Under 60 seconds** unless there is a real reason.
- **Native upload.** Never a YouTube link — LinkedIn suppresses off-platform links.

## 6. The asset problem — the real reason accounts stall

This is the most common, least discussed failure in the entire system.

The pattern is always the same. The content system works. The writing is good. And
then the same six images get recycled for a year, because getting new material out of
a busy founder is harder than writing the posts.

It is normal to ask a founder for photos more often than you ask your own family,
and still not get them.

**The fix is a standing capture habit, not a request.**

### The capture SOP

**Weekly, 10 minutes, one shared folder.** Every person drops in:
- Screen recordings of anything in progress
- Screenshots of work-in-progress, including bad versions
- Photos of the room, the desk, the whiteboard, the team
- Anything a client said that made someone stop

**Monthly, 30 minutes.** One person tags and files it all. Untagged assets do not
exist — you will never find them again.

**Every project, three mandatory captures.** Build them into the process so they
happen without anyone remembering:
1. The first rough version
2. The version you argued about
3. The final

Those three images are a case study, a craft drop and a process post. Three posts
per project, generated automatically, forever.

**Every trip, event or office day: 30 photos minimum.** They cost nothing at the
time and they are the entire supply for the next quarter.

### The rule that makes it stick

**No asset, no post.** If the folder is empty on Monday, that is a supply problem,
and it gets fixed on Monday — not at 8pm on Thursday when someone needs an image.

## 7. What not to attach

- ❌ Stock photography, in any form
- ❌ A screenshot of text you could have written as text
- ❌ A quote card of your own quote from the same post
- ❌ Generic AI-generated illustration — it reads as low effort instantly, and if
  you sell design, it reads as hypocrisy
- ❌ A logo on a coloured background as a "launch" image
- ❌ Anything with a client's confidential data visible. Check twice. See `02-INTAKE.md` §4.
