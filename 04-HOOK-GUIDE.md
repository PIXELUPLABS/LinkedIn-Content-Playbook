# The Hook Guide

The first one to three lines decide whether the rest of the post exists.

LinkedIn shows about two lines before "…see more". Everything you worked on lives
behind a click that most people will not make. **The hook is the highest-leverage
sentence you will write all day**, and it deserves more of your time than the body.

---

## Part 1 — What a hook is actually doing

A hook does one job: **make continuing feel more attractive than scrolling.**

That is a lower bar than "be brilliant" and a higher bar than "be interesting."
The competition is not other posts. The competition is the thumb.

Five things a working hook does. You need two or three, not all five.

**1. Breaks the pattern.**
The reader's brain is scanning for "another one of those." Anything that does not
look like the last twelve posts gets a half-second of real attention. This is why
dialogue, odd numbers and blunt statements work — they do not scan as LinkedIn.

**2. Opens a gap — and closes it fast.**
An unanswered question is uncomfortable, and people read to resolve it. But see
the anti-hook rule in Part 4: you open the gap in line one and close it in line two.
Holding the answer hostage is the single most common way to kill a good post.

**3. Names the reader.**
"If you are a founder under $1M ARR —" is what makes the right person stop and the
wrong person leave. Both of those are wins. Vague hooks are addressed to nobody and
stop nobody.

**4. Carries a number.**
A specific number is a promise that the post contains something real. "$40,000" is
a hook. "A significant project" is not.

**5. Costs something to say.**
Confession, disagreement, a real failure, a number that does not flatter you. The
reader can feel when a sentence was safe to write, and safe sentences get scrolled.

## Part 2 — The data: which hooks actually win

This is where most hook advice falls over. It tells you what sounds good. Here is
what measured.

**Method.** A year of one B2B founder's LinkedIn (~230 posts), each post scored on
whether it broke out — meaning it reached the platform's own top-50 list for that
account. Paid promotion was subtracted first, because boosted posts otherwise look
like popular posts. About **1 in 5 posts** broke out, so that is the bar. Anything
below is a real loser, anything meaningfully above is a real winner.

### Hooks that win REACH

| Hook / format | How it did vs average |
|---|---|
| **Meta-content** — posting about posting, the platform, the industry's own habits | **2.1x** |
| **Meme or humour open** | **1.7x** |
| **Humorous register** overall | **1.6x** |
| **Quote or dialogue cold-open** — starting mid-conversation | **1.6x** |
| Listicle open ("7 things that…") | 1.2x |

### Hooks that win RESPONSE (engagement rate, not reach)

| Hook | How it did |
|---|---|
| **Callout-you** — "Your X is broken" | Best engagement rate of any hook type |
| **Comment-for-link offer** | Highest of all, by a distance |
| **Number-stat open** | Well above average |
| **Data-study open** — "I analysed N things" | Well above average |

### Hooks that LOSE

| Hook / format | How it did |
|---|---|
| **Number-stat open** (for *reach*) | **0.3x** — see the paradox below |
| **Step-by-step teardown / how-to format** | **0.3x** — see the warning below |
| Vulnerable register | Broke out zero times |
| Middle-of-funnel product content | 0.5x |

### The two findings that change how you work

**The number paradox.** Number-stat hooks are in the *worst* list for reach and the
*best* list for response. They rarely break out — but when someone does see one,
they act on it. So: use a number hook when you want the right people to reply, not
when you want a lot of people to see it. Those are different jobs and most people
never separate them.

**The how-to warning — and why the data is misleading here.**
Step-by-step teardowns had the worst reach on the board. Do not conclude they are
bad content. In practice, tactical how-to posts consistently drive the most website
clicks, signups and actual leads, even while losing the reach game badly.

They lose reach and win revenue. If you judge them by impressions you will delete
your best lead source. **Judge a reach post by reach and a utility post by leads.**

### Cross-check: an independent set

A separate study of 559 posts from three well-known B2B creators ranked hooks by
engagement, and it broadly agrees on the winners:

| Rank | Hook type | Template |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Superlative insight | "The biggest [unlock/mistake] between [X] and [Y] wasn't [expected]. It was [unexpected]." |
| 2 | Contrarian / fed up | "I'm sick of [common industry advice]." |
| 3 | Milestone | "[We] just [hit/launched] [specific milestone]." |
| 4 | Conditional advice | "If you're [at stage], stop [common practice]." |
| 5 | Percentage claim | "[High %] of [group] are not [doing something]." |
| 6 | Authority story | "[Impressive person] [did something surprising]." |
| 7 | Data revelation | "The data is in: [surprising statistic]." |

**Where the two datasets disagree:** the external set rates number and percentage
hooks highly; the measured set says they lose reach. Both can be true — the
external study measured engagement, and engagement is exactly where number hooks
are strong. When two datasets disagree, the tiebreaker is always your own account.
See `11-MEASUREMENT.md`.

## Part 3 — Hook length

Shorter wins, and the effect is large.

| Hook length | Result |
|---|---|
| Under ~40 characters | Good |
| **43–78 characters** | **Best — the sweet spot** |
| 79–114 characters | Falling off |
| Over 114 characters | **About half the reach of the sweet spot** |

The same pattern holds for the whole post: posts under ~1,400 characters roughly
**doubled** the median reach of posts over ~3,000. Posts under 15 lines beat posts
over 38 lines.

**43–78 characters is roughly six to twelve words.** One line on a phone.

If your hook needs two lines to land, it is not a hook, it is a first paragraph.

## Part 4 — The anti-hook rules

These are the rules that separate people who understand hooks from people who have
read a listicle about them.

**1. Pay the hook off immediately.**
Line one raises it. Line two answers it. Not paragraph four.

> ✅ "We sent 3,645 messages to see how many calls we could book.
>     The answer was 21."
>
> ❌ "We sent 3,645 messages. You won't believe what happened next."

Withholding the answer feels like it should farm comments. What it actually does is
make people leave, because they have been trained by a decade of clickbait to know
the payoff is not worth it.

**2. Open with a claim, not an open-ended question.**
"Ever wondered why your landing page isn't converting?" reads as filler and gets
scrolled. If you use a question, answer it in the next line.

**3. Never open with "excited to announce" or "thrilled to share."**
Open with the thing itself, or with the pain it kills. Nobody has ever stopped
scrolling because a stranger was excited.

**4. Do not stack qualifiers.**
"In my experience, I've generally found that most of the time…" — every hedge is a
reason to scroll. Say the thing.

**5. Do not explain the hook.**
If you have to add "…and here's why that matters," the hook did not work. Rewrite it.

**6. The first three words carry most of the weight.**
"Most agencies say…" and "Agencies say…" are different hooks. Front-load.

## Part 5 — The twelve hook types

Each one, what it is for, and how it breaks. Filled examples are in
`05-HOOK-LIBRARY.md`.

**1. The number-first receipt**
`[I/we] + [exact number] + [timeframe] — here's the exact [thing]:`
Your credibility arrives before your opinion does. The number does the bragging so
the prose does not have to.
*Breaks when:* the number is round. 617 is believable. 600 is a guess. Never round.

**2. The contrarian slap**
`[Common practice] is [wrong/stupid]. But [most people] do it anyway.`
Works because disagreement is the cheapest form of interest.
*Breaks when:* the take is contrarian about nothing anyone believes. You need a real,
widely-held belief to push against.

**3. The reader callout (the bouncer)**
`[Specific segment]? Read this.` / `If you're [situation], stop [action].`
Names who this is for, and by implication who it is not for. Best engagement rate
of any hook type in the measured data.
*Breaks when:* the segment is too broad. "Founders" is not a segment. "Founders
under $1M ARR with one marketing hire" is.

**4. The dialogue cold-open**
`"[Something someone actually said]"` then your reaction on its own line.
1.6x for reach. It does not scan as a LinkedIn post, which is exactly the point.
*Breaks when:* the quote is invented or polished. Keep it messy and real.

**5. The confession**
`I have a confession. / [Thing that makes you look bad].`
Buys enormous trust, and costs you nothing if the recovery is in the post.
*Breaks when:* there is no fix afterwards. Confession without recovery is just
complaining. Note: pure vulnerability with no lesson broke out zero times in the
measured set.

**6. The superlative insight**
`The biggest [X] between [A] and [B] wasn't [expected]. It was [unexpected].`
Highest engagement in the external dataset. It promises a counterintuitive payoff
in one line.
*Breaks when:* the "unexpected" thing is actually expected.

**7. The before/after jump**
`[Year]: [humble thing] / [Year]: [impressive thing]`
Compresses a long arc into two lines. Very high performing.
*Breaks when:* the gap is not big enough to be interesting, or it reads as bragging
with no lesson attached.

**8. The percentage claim**
`[High %] of [group] are not [doing something].`
Bold enough that the reader checks themselves against it.
*Breaks when:* you cannot say where the number came from. Someone will ask.

**9. The myth autopsy**
`Everyone believes [X]. Here's why that's wrong.`
*Breaks when:* you attack a strawman. Argue against the strongest version.

**10. The meta-observation**
A post about your own industry's habits, the platform, or the way people in your
field behave. **The single best reach ratio in the measured data (2.1x)** and almost
always under-used.
*Breaks when:* it becomes navel-gazing with no value for the reader.

**11. The curation open**
`The [N] best [things] I've seen this [period]:`
Low effort to make, high value to receive, extremely bookmarkable. For a visual
business this is one of the strongest formats available.
*Breaks when:* you curate without a point of view. The selection is the work.

**12. The scene-setter**
`[Time marker], [something happened].`
"Last week a founder sent me a link on Slack." Puts the reader inside a specific
moment instead of a general claim.
*Breaks when:* the time marker is vague. "Recently" is not a time marker.

## Part 6 — How to actually write one

**Write ten. Keep one.** This is the whole method. A hook you wrote first is
almost never your best hook — it is your most obvious one, which means it is also
everyone else's.

The routine that works:

1. Write the post body first, with a placeholder hook. Do not let the hook block
   the writing.
2. Find the most surprising sentence anywhere in the body. It is usually buried in
   the middle. That sentence, alone, is often your real hook.
3. Now write nine more, deliberately using nine different types from Part 5.
4. Cut each to under 78 characters.
5. Read all ten aloud. Delete anything you would not say out loud to a person.
6. Pick the one that would make *you* stop, not the one you are proudest of.

Then run the checklist below.

---

## Part 7 — The hook checklist

Before you post, the hook must pass all seven:

- [ ] Under 78 characters, ideally 43–78
- [ ] Payload delivered by line two — no withheld answer
- [ ] Contains one of: a specific number, a named reader, a real quote, or a claim someone could disagree with
- [ ] No round numbers, no hedges, no "excited to announce"
- [ ] The first three words carry weight
- [ ] It would survive being said out loud to a colleague
- [ ] A stranger who reads only this line still learns something

If it fails two or more, you have a first paragraph, not a hook. Go back to Part 6.

## Part 8 — Diagnosing a hook that did not work

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| High impressions, no engagement | The hook was interesting, the post was not | The body did not pay off the promise. Tighten the middle. |
| Low impressions, high engagement rate | The hook was too narrow, or number-led | Fine, if it was a utility post. Check bookmarks and DMs before calling it a failure. |
| Both low | The hook was generic | Rewrite using a different type from Part 5. Do not tweak — replace. |
| Good numbers, wrong audience | The hook appealed to peers, not buyers | Add the reader callout. Exclude out loud. |
| Comments arguing about a side point | The hook made two claims | One argument per post. Split it. |

**The most common failure by a distance is a hook that describes the topic instead
of making the point.** "Some thoughts on hiring designers" is a topic. "The best
designer I ever hired failed the trial" is a hook.
