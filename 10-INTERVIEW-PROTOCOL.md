# The Interview Protocol

**This is the engine.** Everything else in the pack is a tool. This is the habit
that keeps the tools supplied.

The problem it solves: a founder has all the material and none of the time. Asking
them to write is asking for the one thing they will never give you. Asking them to
*talk for 45 minutes every two weeks* is a meeting, and they already do meetings.

---

## The ritual

**Every two weeks. 45 minutes. Recorded.**

Put it in the calendar as a recurring event with a name that sounds important,
because it is. Never let it become "when we have time."

### What you need
- Any recorder that captures decent audio and gives you a transcript
- A camera if you want short-form video clips as a by-product (worth it)
- Ten questions from `09-QUESTION-BANK.md`, chosen in advance and **not shared**

### Why not share the questions
Prepared answers are worse. They come out as press releases. The value is in the
unrehearsed specificity — the number they remember off the top of their head, the
client they name without thinking, the thing they say and then say "well, off the
record" about. Prepared founders give you the website copy. Ambushed founders give
you content.

---

## How to run the 45 minutes

**Minutes 0–5. Warm up.**
Ask about the week. Do not record content yet. You are getting them talking normally
instead of presenting.

**Minutes 5–35. Six to eight questions.**
One question, then get out of the way.

Three interviewer rules, and they are the whole skill:

1. **Do not fill silence.** The best answer usually arrives after an uncomfortable
   pause. If you rescue them at three seconds, you get the surface answer.
2. **Ask "what happened?" not "what do you think?"** Opinions are cheap and generic.
   Stories contain the numbers, the names and the detail that make content credible.
3. **Chase the specific.** Every time they generalise, pull them back:
   - "When was that?"
   - "How much?"
   - "Who was it?"
   - "What did they actually say?"
   - "Give me the example."

**Minutes 35–45. The gold round.**
These four questions consistently produce the best material. Ask them last, when
they are warmed up and slightly bored of being careful:

- "What's something you believe about this that would annoy people in our industry?"
- "What's the thing we're getting wrong right now?"
- "What did a client say to you recently that stuck?"
- "What are you avoiding thinking about?"

## What happens after

One recording produces four different assets. Most teams take one and waste the
other three.

| Output | What it is | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Transcript** | The raw material for written posts | `prompts/interview.md`, then `prompts/draft-post.md` |
| **Clips** | 30–90 second vertical cuts of the best answers | Short-form video, and native LinkedIn video |
| **Quotes** | Three or four lines worth using verbatim | Hooks, quote cards, carousel slides |
| **New questions** | The things they said that you did not understand | Next session |

### Turning the transcript into posts

Run `prompts/interview.md`. It extracts **rules**, not stories.

This distinction is the whole quality bar:

> **A story is not an insight. It is the proof of one.**
>
> ❌ "We had a client who wanted to skip strategy and it went badly."
> ✅ "Clients who skip the strategy phase spend the savings twice in revisions —
>    here's the project where we measured it."
>
> The first is an anecdote. The second is a rule a stranger can act on, with the
> anecdote attached as evidence.

For each extracted rule, capture four things:
1. **The rule** — one sentence a stranger could act on
2. **Why it's true** — the mechanism, not just the claim
3. **The receipt** — the verbatim quote, the number, the named example
4. **When it breaks** — the conditions where the rule stops holding

Rule four is what separates a real insight from a LinkedIn platitude. Anyone can
state a rule. Knowing where it fails is the expertise.

---

## Yield

A 45-minute session, run properly, should give you:

- **6–10 extractable rules**
- **3–5 finished written posts**
- **2–4 video clips**
- **1 carousel**
- **Several hooks** you would never have invented at a desk

That is two to three weeks of content from one meeting. Which is exactly the point:
**you are not asking a founder for content, you are asking for 45 minutes.**

---

## Keep a bank, not a queue

File every extracted rule in one place, with:
- a conviction score — how many *separate* sessions this idea has come up in
- a status — used or unused

**Sort by conviction and use unused ones first.** An idea a founder has repeated
across three sessions unprompted is their actual belief, and it will be their most
convincing content. An idea they said once might have been a mood.

**Mark things used.** A bank nobody marks becomes a graveyard, and then everyone
stops opening it.

---

## If the founder will not sit down

It happens, and it is the most common failure of the whole system. Fallbacks, in
order of quality:

1. **Ride along on a call they were already having.** A sales call, an internal
   review, a client kickoff. Record it. Real conversations beat interviews.
2. **Voice notes.** Send one question at a time. A two-minute reply is enough for
   one post.
3. **Ask over text and screenshot the reply.** Their unedited written answer is
   already in their voice, which is most of the battle.
4. **Mine what already exists.** Old calls, old decks, Slack messages, their replies
   in other people's comments. There is more material in the building than anyone
   thinks.

But say the quiet part out loud to them once, plainly:

> "You can pay me whatever you want. If you don't give me 45 minutes a fortnight,
> this will not work, and I'd rather tell you now than in month four."

That conversation is uncomfortable. Not having it is worse, because the system will
quietly fail and everyone will blame the content.
